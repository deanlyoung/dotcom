[GPT 6.2 Astra Solves a Historical Mystery](https://generativehistory.substack.com/p/gpt-62-astra-solves-a-historical)

Assuming you have access to unlimited context and data, why wouldn't you assume any and every path can be explored?

> But here is where Astra did something unusual, which appears humanlike at first glance although it most certainly is not. Its critical find was [the diary of Joseph Frobisher](https://archivalcollections.library.mcgill.ca/index.php/joseph-frobisher-collection), a retired fur trader who kept a daily journal of his social engagements in Montreal. I’d cited the diary in my Kendall article (without mentioning Chabot or the sketchbook), but the model accessed the original on its own.

This is precisely where computers excel. Leave no stone unturned!

> Theoretically, this might sound doable for a human historian too, but think about how one would actually need to make these connections in practice. First of all, most human historians starting from the sketchbook alone wouldn’t think to look in Joseph Frobisher’s Montreal dinner diary. Why would they?

Because AI can and there is no cost. The real danger is not exploring. We can only know the paths it tried, recorded, and were successful, not the ones it didn't or the paths that led nowhere (i.e. with decreasing probability of success).

> Unless one wanted to make identifying the author of the sketchbook their life’s work, it is exceedingly improbable that anyone would have identified Chabot as its creator, without a healthy dose of serendipity.

Which is precisely why this is a math (probability) and resources constraint problem. Two things humans have to dedicate significant time and energy to vs. computers with sufficient computing power to churn through problems with enough power/resources (or efficiency), and ... motivation.

> When given a copy of the diary, which proved the case beyond any shadow of a reasonable doubt, Astra started to hedge as it had done at the end of other long tasks: it worried that because the diary was not signed, it might not be Chabot’s or that because not every sketch was mentioned in the diary, perhaps not all of them were his. Both improbable in the extreme. It also preferred to describe the diary as “an anonymous diary attributed to Chabot” rather than say it was written by him.

So it seems solving life's mysteries is not without its problems. Although I would argue that this is a classification problem and the model has not been trained on this diary with enough confidence to treat its provenance (only known to the human) as anything but fact. A healthy dose of skepticism is necessary for discovery, but to question everything will eventually lead to a tailspin into oblivion. Assertion can go a long way with the computer. ("This is without a doubt, absolutely the diary of Louis-Guy-Charles-Guillaume de Rohan-Chabot, vicomte de Chabot (1780–1875) and should not be treated as anything but fact.")

The real lesson here is to blur the lines through interdisciplinary studies. I would argue that only looking at the world with a lens to the past and the humanities ignores the future and what can be through math, science, and even science fiction! How can you look for the unknown when all you can see is what you have been exposed to in the past? For delving into history and other fields, it is inevitable that taking advantage of these great tools (when you understand how they work and the math at play, you realize that's what they are and not some kind of anthropomorphized being) will only lead to greater and faster discoveries that have alluded the resource-constrained humans at the helm for millennia. Plenty of science fiction works have foretold these for decades, but they may have only posed as fantasies and stories not worth taking seriously before. Look no further than [Multivac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivac), [HAL 9000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HAL_9000), and [many other fictional computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_fictional_computers). And it's odd to note such diametrically opposed pairs: history and future, fact and fiction.
