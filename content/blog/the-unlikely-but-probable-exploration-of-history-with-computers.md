Mark Humphries, [GPT 6.2 Astra Solves a Historical Mystery](https://generativehistory.substack.com/p/gpt-62-astra-solves-a-historical)

Assuming you have access to unlimited context and data, why wouldn't you assume any and every path can be explored?

> But here is where Astra did something unusual, which appears humanlike at first glance although it most certainly is not. Its critical find was [the diary of Joseph Frobisher](https://archivalcollections.library.mcgill.ca/index.php/joseph-frobisher-collection), a retired fur trader who kept a daily journal of his social engagements in Montreal. I’d cited the diary in my Kendall article (without mentioning Chabot or the sketchbook), but the model accessed the original on its own.

This is precisely where computers excel. Leave no stone unturned!

> Theoretically, this might sound doable for a human historian too, but think about how one would actually need to make these connections in practice. First of all, most human historians starting from the sketchbook alone wouldn’t think to look in Joseph Frobisher’s Montreal dinner diary. Why would they?

Because AI can, and the cost of checking one more lead can be low. There’s a cost to leaving leads unexplored, too. We can see the paths the model reports, especially the ones that worked. How many trails went cold? How many did it never try?

> Unless one wanted to make identifying the author of the sketchbook their life’s work, it is exceedingly improbable that anyone would have identified Chabot as its creator, without a healthy dose of serendipity.

Which is precisely why I see this as a problem of probability and resources. A historian has to decide whether a long shot is worth the hours it might take. A computer with enough processing power and access to the archives can chase more of those leads. It still needs someone to ask the question and decide how far to follow it.

> When given a copy of the diary, which proved the case beyond any shadow of a reasonable doubt, Astra started to hedge as it had done at the end of other long tasks: it worried that because the diary was not signed, it might not be Chabot’s or that because not every sketch was mentioned in the diary, perhaps not all of them were his. Both improbable in the extreme. It also preferred to describe the diary as “an anonymous diary attributed to Chabot” rather than say it was written by him.

So it seems solving life's mysteries comes with its own problems. I’d argue this is a classification problem. Humphries treats the diary’s provenance as established, while Astra keeps revisiting the attribution. A healthy dose of skepticism is necessary for discovery, but to question everything will eventually lead to a tailspin into oblivion. In November 2026, for the behavior Humphries describes in GPT 6.2 Astra, I’d try giving the computer an explicit premise. “For this analysis, treat the attribution of the diary to Louis-Guy-Charles-Guillaume de Rohan-Chabot, vicomte de Chabot (1780–1875), as established.”

The real lesson here is to blur the lines through interdisciplinary studies. In archival research, math and computing can change which sources you have time to check. But a Montreal dinner diary might put Chabot in the right company without telling you who drew a particular sketch. The wider the search, the more chances you have of finding a coincidence that looks like evidence. I’d want a historian to read the entry alongside the sketchbook and check what the two actually establish.

How can you look for the unknown when all you can see is what you have been exposed to in the past? That’s where even science fiction comes in for me. It gives us ways to imagine what we might ask a computer to do, and what happens when we trust its answer. Look no further than [Multivac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivac), [HAL 9000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HAL_9000), and [many other fictional computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_fictional_computers).
